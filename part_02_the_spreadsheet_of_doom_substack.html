The Spreadsheet of Doom

A story about a $2 billion supply chain, a man named Dennis, and a file that Excel keeps trying to kill

This is a work of fiction. Any resemblance to actual companies, living or automated, is entirely coincidental and also probably their fault.

The spreadsheet was called PROC_MASTER_v2_FINAL_FINAL_USE_THIS_ONE(3).xlsx.

It was 147 megabytes. It had 94 tabs. It contained 380,000 rows of procurement data spanning fifteen years, four ERP migrations, and the complete emotional arc of a man named Dennis Trauber, who had built it in 2011 because his manager at the time had said, "Can you just throw something together until we get the real system?"

The real system never came. Dennis was still there. The spreadsheet was still there. Together, they routed $2.1 billion in annual procurement across 1,400 vendors, 38 countries, and a materials taxonomy that Dennis kept in his head because the dropdown menus had run out of room in 2016.

It was, by any sane measure, the most dangerous file in the company. It was also the only thing that worked.

MegaCorp had not always been MegaCorp. It had been Mercer Industrial, founded in 1947 by a mechanical engineer named Elias Mercer who had built turbines for the Navy and who believed, with the fervor of a man who had seen what bad engineering could do to a ship, that the product was the company. He hired engineers first and accountants second. He walked the factory floor daily. He knew the names of the machinists and the tolerances of the castings and the sound of a turbine that was right. The company that bore his name was, for three decades, the kind of place where people like Gary were not called "legacy knowledge holders." They were called "engineers." The name changed in 1991 when a private equity consortium acquired the company, merged it with two competitors, and rechristened it MegaCorp Industries. Elias Mercer was dead by then. The founder's great-granddaughter would later run the company. Her father — Elias's grandson — sat on the board for four years before selling his shares. The product hadn't changed. The culture had. Slowly. The way a river changes course — not with a dramatic event, but with the patient, daily accumulation of sediment in places nobody was watching.

Dennis ate lunch in the Plant 4 break room because the cafeteria on the main campus served something called a "wellness bowl" that cost $14 and tasted like ambition. The break room had a vending machine that sold coffee for seventy-five cents. Dennis had been drinking that coffee since the vending machine was installed. He knew the machine personally. He could tell by the sound of the pump whether the coffee would be merely bad or actively hostile.

He and Gary Kowalski used to eat lunch together. Same table, same time, for thirteen years. Gary had the ham sandwich. Dennis had the coffee and whatever his wife had packed, which was always better than anything the campus offered and which Dennis never acknowledged because acknowledging it would require a conversation about feelings, and Dennis did not have conversations about feelings. He had conversations about lead times.

Here's the thing about Dennis: he could have documented it. Any time in fifteen years. He could have written down the weather suffixes, the vendor relationships, the logic behind the conditional formatting. Hank at Hartfield had done it — three-ring binder, numbered tabs, because it was the right thing to do. Dennis had not. Not because he was lazy. Because the spreadsheet was his. The undocumented complexity was the thing that made him necessary, and Dennis, for all his pragmatism, needed to be necessary. A small, human, entirely understandable form of hoarding — the quiet refusal to make yourself replaceable in a company that was always looking for reasons to replace you. Gary had done the same thing with his notebooks. The knowledge stayed in the knower not just because the institution didn't ask for it, but because the knower, on some level, didn't want to let it go. The entropy was inside him too.

Gary was gone now. Retired. "Voluntarily," the way jumping out of a burning building is voluntary. Dennis had watched the whole thing — the knowledge extraction, the platform, the hoodie. He'd said nothing at the time because Dennis did not say things at the time. He said things in spreadsheet comments, months later, in Column BQ, which nobody read. Column BQ of the procurement master was, in effect, Dennis's diary. Cell BQ-14072 read: Gary knew the washers were wrong. They fired him for knowing. They didn't fire him. They thanked him. That's worse.

Nobody had ever read Column BQ. The column header said NOTES (INTERNAL — DO NOT DELETE). IT had tried to delete it during the 2019 data cleanup initiative. The cleanup initiative had crashed the file. Dennis had restored it from a backup he kept on a USB drive in his desk drawer, inside a coffee mug that said "World's Most Adequate Employee." The mug had been a gift from Gary.

The CEO's "AI-First Transformation" arrived at procurement on a Tuesday. You could see it coming. You couldn't stop it. Someone was going to lose a roof.

Her name was Jacqueline Mercer, and she had been CEO for fourteen months, which at MegaCorp was long enough to have commissioned two McKinsey studies and short enough to still believe the second one. She was not stupid. She had built and sold a supply chain analytics startup in her thirties, survived two PE-backed turnarounds, and once fired a CFO mid-earnings call for lying about inventory reserves. She understood supply chains at a level of abstraction that was simultaneously impressive and exactly wrong for the problem at hand.

Because Jacqueline's understanding of procurement was strategic. It operated at the level of vendor portfolios and category management and total cost of ownership models that looked beautiful on slides. What it did not operate at was the level of Dennis's spreadsheet, which is to say: the level where things actually happened.

This isn't just a technology upgrade — it's a fundamental reimagining of how we create value across the supply ecosystem. The future of procurement is intelligent, autonomous, and deeply aligned with business outcomes. Proud to be part of this journey. #AIFirst #ProcurementExcellence #TransformationInAction

— Fatima Hussaini, VP of Strategic Sourcing & Procurement Modernization, MegaCorp Industries

Sourcient AI was a three-year-old startup out of Susquehanna Valley that had raised $85 million on the premise that large language models could "reason about supply chain complexity." Their demo was extraordinary. You could ask it questions in natural language — "Which vendors have the highest risk of disruption in Q3?" — and it would produce answers that sounded confident and looked authoritative and were, in Dennis's professional assessment, "about 60% right, which in procurement is the same as 100% wrong."

Sixty percent accuracy in procurement meant you were right about the paper clips and wrong about the titanium forgings. It meant the AI could tell you that your office supply vendor was stable (correct, also irrelevant) while missing that your sole-source provider of J-7 turbine housings was six weeks from bankruptcy because their CFO had been embezzling since 2021 and the receivables on their balance sheet were fictional. Dennis knew about the receivables because he called his vendors. Not emailed. Called. On the phone. Like it was 1987. He could hear the hesitation in a procurement manager's voice the way Gary could hear a line running wrong. Some signals don't survive digitization.

The implementation team arrived in force. Twelve consultants, four Sourcient engineers, a "change management specialist" whose job appeared to consist entirely of scheduling meetings about the meetings, and a project manager whose Gantt chart was a work of art that bore the same relationship to reality that a map of Middle-earth bears to actual geography.

Their first task was to "ingest Dennis's data."

The ingestion meeting was held in Conference Room 7B, which seated twelve but contained nineteen people because the transformation had attracted stakeholders who could smell budget from three floors away. Dennis sat in the corner with his laptop open. The spreadsheet was on his screen. It had been on his screen, in some form, for fifteen years.

"So, Dennis," said the Sourcient lead, a young man named Archer who had the energy of someone who had recently discovered matcha and wanted to tell you about it. "Walk us through the data model."

"There isn't a data model," Dennis said.

"The... architecture, then. The schema."

"There isn't a schema."

Archer's smile held. Barely. "Okay. Let's start with the tabs. What does each tab represent?"

"Depends on the tab."

"Can you give us an example?"

Dennis clicked to a tab called VENDOR_RISK_DO_NOT_TOUCH. "This one tracks vendor financial health. I update it quarterly based on phone calls, credit reports, and whether their invoices start arriving late, which is usually the first sign."

"And where does the data feed from?"

"My phone."

Archer wrote something on his tablet. Dennis suspected it was not complimentary.

They spent four hours walking through the spreadsheet. By the end, the Sourcient team had identified what they called "critical data gaps" and what Dennis called "things I keep in my head because the spreadsheet ran out of columns." The materials taxonomy alone — the classification system for every component MegaCorp bought — existed as a combination of dropdown menus, color-coded cells, conditional formatting rules that triggered other conditional formatting rules, and a naming convention that Dennis had invented and that used the first two letters of the vendor's city, the last three digits of the part number, and a suffix that indicated risk level using a system based, improbably, on weather metaphors. A "sunny" suffix meant the vendor was reliable. A "tornado" suffix meant call immediately. A "fog" suffix meant Dennis wasn't sure yet, and a "fog" suffix on a sole-source vendor meant Dennis was losing sleep.

"This is incredible," said Archer, in the tone of someone seeing a car crash from a helicopter.

"It works," said Dennis.

"But it's not scalable."

"It scales to $2.1 billion."

"But it's not repeatable."

"I've been repeating it for fifteen years."

Archer had the expression of a man bringing civilization to someone who didn't realize they needed it. Dennis had the expression of a man who had seen this expression before, on the faces of the last four people who had tried to replace his spreadsheet. The first had been a SAP implementation in 2014 that ran $12 million over budget and was abandoned after the go-live crashed during a quarter-end close. The second was an Oracle procurement module in 2017 that technically worked but required seventeen clicks to create a purchase order that Dennis could create in two. The third was a "best of breed" startup in 2019 that pivoted to crypto six months into the engagement. The fourth was Sourcient AI, which was happening now, and which Dennis regarded with the weary patience of a man who knew he would outlast it.

What Dennis did not know — what he could not know, because the information existed in only two heads and one encrypted email — was that the AI transformation had a secondary objective. The official goal was "modernize procurement through AI-driven intelligence." The unofficial goal was to document Dennis's processes thoroughly enough that Dennis himself became optional.

The person who had pushed hardest for this was not Jacqueline Mercer. It was Meredith Liang, VP of Knowledge Management — a role that MegaCorp had created after Gary's departure when someone on the board finally asked, "How many other Garys do we have?" The answer, once Meredith was hired to find out, was: at least forty. Forty people across MegaCorp whose retirement would take irreplaceable knowledge out the door. Dennis was number three on her list.

Meredith was not a villain. She was, in fact, the person at MegaCorp who most clearly saw the problem Gary represented. She had read the post-mortem on the turbine crisis. She had pulled Dennis's personnel file and seen that he was fifty-eight, had not documented his processes, and sat at the center of a $2.1 billion supply chain like a keystone in an arch that nobody had bothered to reinforce. She understood, with genuine alarm, that MegaCorp was one retirement party away from another catastrophe.

Her solution was Sourcient. Not because she was naive about AI — she had a PhD in organizational learning from Carnegie Mellon and she had read enough to know the limits. But she also knew that the alternative was doing nothing, and doing nothing was what MegaCorp had done with Gary, and Gary's knowledge was now in a cardboard box in a garage. She chose the imperfect option over the absent one. Her slide deck — "Institutional Knowledge at Risk: A Preservation Strategy" — was sincere, well-researched, and quietly devastating. It showed the actuarial curve of MegaCorp's most experienced employees against the documentation rate of their processes. The curves crossed in 2027. The gap between what MegaCorp knew and what MegaCorp had written down was widening at the rate of approximately four retirements per quarter, and each retirement took with it an average of twenty-three years of undocumented expertise.

She was right about the diagnosis. She was wrong about the solution. She knew she might be wrong about the solution. She did it anyway, because the diagnosis was urgent and the alternative was a spreadsheet in a coffee mug and a hope that Dennis would live forever. This is what made her tragic — not ignorance, not greed, but the particular anguish of someone who sees the problem clearly and reaches for the best tool available and watches the tool do exactly the wrong thing with exactly the right data.

Sourcient's AI was trained on Dennis's data over the following six weeks. The spreadsheet was parsed, cleaned, normalized, and loaded into what Sourcient called a "procurement intelligence graph" — a knowledge representation that mapped vendors, materials, contracts, risk scores, and relationships into a queryable structure that could be accessed by anyone with the right permissions.

The knowledge graph was, in fairness, technically impressive. It represented Dennis's fifteen years of work as a network of 140,000 nodes and 2.3 million edges. It could answer questions. It could generate reports. It could visualize supply chain dependencies as interactive diagrams that made executives feel informed.

What it could not do was call Hector Delgado at World Precision Machining on a Friday afternoon and hear in his voice that the shop floor was running hot because they'd just lost their best CNC operator to a competitor and the quality on the next shipment of turbine housings was going to be marginal, not technically out of spec but close enough that Gary would have caught it if Gary were still there, and Dennis should maybe hold an extra week of safety stock just in case.

The knowledge graph did not know about Hector. It did not know about Friday afternoons, or the slight tightness in a man's voice when he's worried but can't say why because his own procurement people are listening. It did not know that "on time and in spec" sometimes means "barely, and only if you don't look too closely." It did not know that Dennis's weather suffixes — sunny, fog, tornado — were not a quaint legacy system but a compression algorithm for human judgment, and that the information they encoded had saved MegaCorp from at least $40 million in supplier failures over the last decade, a figure that nobody knew because nobody had ever asked Dennis to quantify what didn't go wrong.

Dennis tried to explain this. Once. In a meeting. The change management specialist nodded empathetically and typed "stakeholder resistance — address in sprint 4."

Winners to be announced at the annual gala in Scottsdale. Regardless of the outcome, this recognition validates the transformative work happening across our industry. #Procurement #Innovation #Blessed

— Gavin Royce, Director of Procurement Analytics, MegaCorp Industries

Gavin had been at MegaCorp for eleven months. He had never spoken to Dennis. He had never opened the spreadsheet. He had, however, given three internal presentations about the Sourcient implementation, each featuring a slide titled "From Tribal Knowledge to Institutional Intelligence." The slide had a picture of a brain being uploaded into a cloud. It did not feature a picture of Dennis.

The failure, when it came, arrived not as a catastrophe but as a series of small incorrectnesses that compounded the way interest compounds — invisibly, then suddenly.

Sourcient's AI recommended a vendor consolidation. This was one of its core value propositions: identify redundant vendors, consolidate spend, negotiate volume discounts. The AI identified seventeen vendors that could be consolidated into six. The projected savings were $4.2 million annually. The presentation was forty slides. The math was clean. Jacqueline approved it.

Dennis saw the recommendation and felt his stomach drop. Someone had just said "we don't really need that column, do we?" — except it wasn't a column. It was three vendors. Because three of the vendors being eliminated were sole-source backups. Not primary suppliers — backups. Companies that Dennis maintained relationships with not because MegaCorp bought from them regularly, but because MegaCorp might need to buy from them urgently. They were Dennis's tornado insurance. He'd spent years cultivating them — small orders, annual check-ins, the occasional expedited job that kept MegaCorp on their radar as a customer worth prioritizing in an emergency.

The AI didn't know they were insurance. The AI saw low transaction volume and high per-unit cost. The AI saw inefficiency. Dennis saw a safety net being cut by someone who had never needed to use it and therefore concluded it served no purpose.

He flagged it. In an email to Gavin Royce, cc'ing the Sourcient team. Subject line: "Vendor consolidation risk — sole source backups." The email was three paragraphs. It was clear, factual, and written by someone who had been writing emails for thirty years and had learned to keep the anger in Column BQ. The ancient curse was to see the future and have nobody believe you. The modern version is to see the future and have nobody read your email.

Gavin forwarded it to the change management specialist with the note: "FYI — some pushback from legacy team. Can you loop in?"

The change management specialist scheduled a meeting. Dennis attended. He made his case. The Sourcient lead countered with data. The data was correct and irrelevant, like a weather forecast for the wrong city. Dennis said, "The data doesn't know what it doesn't know." The Sourcient lead said, "That's why we have the AI — to surface the unknowns." Dennis wanted to say that an AI surfacing unknowns from data that didn't contain the unknowns was like fishing in a swimming pool and concluding the ocean had no fish. What he actually said was, "Okay." He went back to his desk. He opened Column BQ. Cell BQ-31847: They're going to cut the backups. When the next sole-source failure hits, nobody will remember this meeting. I will. It will take 8-12 weeks to requalify. They'll blame the vendor.

The sole-source failure hit seven months later. A titanium forging supplier in South Korea — the only qualified source for a critical turbine component — suffered a factory fire. Nobody was hurt. The factory was not destroyed. But production was down for an estimated ten weeks.

Under the old model — Dennis's model — MegaCorp would have called Boruck Precision in Wisconsin. Boruck was one of the three backup vendors that Sourcient had recommended eliminating. Dennis had kept them warm for nine years. Small orders. Annual visits. A Christmas card that Dennis's wife wrote because Dennis's handwriting was, by his own admission, "forensically indecipherable." Boruck could have had emergency production running in three weeks.

Boruck had been deconsolidated five months ago. Their MegaCorp account was closed. The relationship that had taken Dennis nine years to build had been ended with an automated email from MegaCorp's new vendor management platform. The email began with "Dear Valued Partner" and ended with "We wish you continued success." Boruck's owner, a woman named Donna Boruck whose father had started the shop in 1978, had read it and filed it in a drawer she didn't open again.

MegaCorp's supply chain team scrambled. Sourcient's AI recommended alternative suppliers based on capability matching. The alternatives were technically qualified. They were also six to fourteen months from being able to deliver. In procurement, the difference between "qualified" and "able to deliver in the required timeframe" is the difference between a map of a restaurant and a meal.

Production stopped. Again. This time the loss was $34 million over twelve weeks. A municipal water treatment plant in Dayton that had been waiting eight months for a replacement turbine component was told to expect another twelve weeks. The plant's chief engineer, a woman who had been running the facility for twenty years, called MegaCorp's customer service line. She was on hold for forty minutes. She was transferred three times. She explained that the delay meant running the backup system beyond its rated duty cycle, and that if the backup failed, 140,000 people would be on a boil-water advisory. The customer service representative created a ticket. The ticket was classified as "standard priority." Before the call disconnected, an automated voice asked if she would be willing to participate in a brief survey to help MegaCorp "continue to deliver an exceptional customer experience." She hung up. The system recorded this as "survey declined" and flagged her account for a follow-up satisfaction email, which arrived three days later with the subject line "We value your feedback!" and a coupon code for 10% off MegaCorp's online merchandise store. The store's bestseller was the alumni hoodie. Nobody at MegaCorp ever connected the ticket to the Sourcient vendor consolidation.

The chief engineer solved her own problem. She spent a weekend calling machine shops, working from a supplier directory she'd pulled off an industry forum, until she found a small precision shop in Wisconsin that had recently lost a major client and had capacity. She drove up on a Monday morning, still wearing the MegaCorp hoodie she'd ordered with the coupon because her regular jacket was at the dry cleaner and it was the only clean thing with a zipper. Donna Boruck saw the logo, said nothing for a full three seconds, and then let her in. Boruck had the component machined in nineteen days. The same shop. The same capability. The same three-week turnaround Dennis had always known they could do. The relationship that Sourcient's AI had classified as "redundant" and terminated with an automated email was rebuilt by a municipal engineer in a promotional hoodie, on her own time, because the system that was supposed to work didn't, and the people it had discarded still could.

Entropy doesn't respect company boundaries. Neither do consequences. Neither, apparently, do hoodies.

The CNBC chyron read "MEGACORP: SUPPLY CHAIN DISRUPTION RAISES QUESTIONS ABOUT AI STRATEGY." A16Z published a blog post arguing the crisis proved the need for more AI investment. It got 60,000 likes.

Dennis did not say "I told you so." He did not need to. Cell BQ-31847 said it for him. Nobody read it. Nobody would ever read it. Column BQ was the most accurate audit trail in the company and it existed in a file that the CTO described, in an all-hands Q&A, as "the kind of legacy dependency we're moving beyond."

Meredith Liang read Cell BQ-31847. She was the only person at MegaCorp who ever did. She had asked Dennis to walk her through the spreadsheet — not the Sourcient team, not the ingestion consultants, just her, one afternoon, with coffee from the vending machine because Dennis didn't trust anyone who drank the wellness bowls. She had listened. She had asked about the weather suffixes. She had understood — with a horror that only comes from comprehending both sides of a catastrophe — that the system she had championed to preserve knowledge like Dennis's had just destroyed it. Not the data — the data was in the knowledge graph. The judgment. The Christmas cards. The Friday calls. The nine years of keeping Boruck warm. That was gone, and her platform had cut it, and she had written the business case that authorized the cut.

She did not sleep well for three weeks. She did not tell anyone. She updated her risk assessment. She did not update her sleep. She added "relationship-dependent supply chain intelligence" as a category. She added Dennis's weather suffix system to the knowledge preservation taxonomy she was building. She kept doing the work. Because the alternative was not doing the work, and not doing the work was how Gary's notebook ended up in a box.

Meredith Liang's tragedy was simple: she was the closest thing MegaCorp had to someone who was right, and the tools she had access to were incapable of doing the right thing. She was trying to solve Gary's problem from the institutional side, with institutional instruments, and the instruments were blunt where they needed to be sharp and sharp where they needed to be gentle. She would appear again. The problem she was trying to solve would outlast every tool she applied to it. It would, in fact, outlast the species.

Somewhere in Ohio, Priya Sivaramakrishnan read about the supply chain failure on her lunch break. She was eating at her desk, which at Hartfield Manufacturing was a wooden table in a shared office with three other engineers and a guy named Hank who had been there since the Reagan administration. Hank was eating a sandwich. Priya was eating leftover dal. Neither of them had an AI assistant.

Hartfield's procurement system was a database that Hank had built in Microsoft Access in 2003. It was ugly. It was slow. It had a user interface that looked like it was designed during the Clinton administration because it was. It tracked every vendor, every part, every lead time, and every backup supplier in a structure that Hank could explain in ten minutes because Hank had documented it, in a three-ring binder, with numbered tabs and handwritten annotations, because Hank was the kind of person who documented things not because anyone asked but because it was the right thing to do.

Priya had asked Hank once why he'd documented it so thoroughly. Hank had looked at her like she'd asked why he wore a seatbelt. "Because someday I won't be here," he said. "And whoever comes after me shouldn't have to guess."

Production at Hartfield had not stopped in six years. Their total IT spend was roughly what MegaCorp spent on the kombucha tap in Building 3.

Priya thought about calling Dennis. She still had his number. He'd given it to her the same way Gary had — quietly, at the door, on her last day. No ceremony. Just a number written on a Post-it note. "In case," Dennis had said. The same words Gary had used. The same Post-it note. The same knowledge that the institution wouldn't call, so someone should.

She didn't call. Not yet. She put the Post-it back in her wallet, next to the one from Gary, next to a photo of her parents. Three pieces of paper. All analog. All load-bearing.

Priya read about the supply chain failure and felt vindicated, and the vindication was ungenerous. Somewhere along the way she had stopped believing that anyone at MegaCorp was trying — not the executives, she'd never expected much from them, but the people in the middle. The Meredith Liangs. The managers who shielded their teams. The engineers who stayed. Leaving had hardened something in her. Dennis had stayed. Dennis was trying. Dennis was training the AI that was going to replace him, and he was doing it carefully, because he cared about the work even when the work didn't care about him. Priya, from the clean distance of Hartfield Manufacturing and Hank's documented binder, had the luxury of contempt. She had earned the exit. She had not earned the judgment. But she carried it anyway — the conviction that she would have done better, if only she'd been somewhere else. We all carry that one.

Jacqueline Mercer did not fire anyone over the supply chain failure. This was not mercy. It was strategy. Firing someone would imply that a decision had been wrong, and wrong decisions required accountability, and accountability required acknowledging that the AI-first transformation had a failure mode that looked exactly like the failure mode it was supposed to prevent.

Instead, she commissioned a "lessons learned retrospective." The retrospective concluded that the vendor consolidation had been "directionally correct but insufficiently risk-weighted." Sourcient was given a $1.8 million contract extension to add "supply chain resilience modeling" to their platform. The modeling would, going forward, account for sole-source backup relationships. It would do this by ingesting data about sole-source backup relationships. The data would come from whoever currently tracked sole-source backup relationships.

That person was Dennis.

Dennis was now training the AI to do the thing he had told them the AI couldn't do, using the knowledge they had dismissed when he tried to share it, so that the system could eventually function without him, at which point he would be offered a retirement package and thanked for his contributions.

He recognized the pattern. He'd watched it happen to Gary. The extraction. The platform. The handshake at the door. The quiet removal of the person whose only crime was knowing too much to be easily replaced and not enough to be considered irreplaceable.

He went home that evening and, for the first time in fifteen years, he opened a new spreadsheet. Not for MegaCorp. For himself. He began documenting — not the procurement data, which belonged to the company, but the logic. The reasoning. The why behind every weather suffix, every backup vendor relationship, every conditional formatting rule that contained a judgment call compressed into a color. He titled the file HOW_IT_ACTUALLY_WORKS.xlsx. He saved it to the same USB drive, in the same coffee mug, in his desk drawer.

It was not an act of rebellion. It was something quieter. A man who had watched his friend's knowledge get extracted and commodified and sold, deciding that if the same thing was going to happen to him, at least somewhere there would be a record that understood the difference between the data and the judgment. Between the spreadsheet and the man who made it mean something.

Column A of the new file was labeled WHAT THE AI WILL TELL YOU. Column B was labeled WHAT IT ACTUALLY MEANS. Column C was labeled WHY.

Column C was the one that mattered. Column C was the one the AI would never have.

Dennis is still at MegaCorp. The spreadsheet is still at MegaCorp. Both are load-bearing. Neither appears on the digital transformation roadmap. The Sourcient implementation is now in "Phase 3: Autonomous Procurement Intelligence," which means the AI can generate purchase orders without human approval for transactions under $50,000. The threshold was originally $10,000. It was raised three times in six months because the approval queue kept backing up, and the person who had been approving orders manually — Dennis — was supposed to be focusing on "knowledge transfer," not doing the job the AI was supposed to have learned.

The AI has not learned. It has memorized. There is a difference, but the difference doesn't show up on a dashboard, so it doesn't exist.

Dennis's desk still has the coffee mug. The mug still has the USB drive. The USB drive now has two files: the backup of the procurement master and the new one. The one that explains why.

Gary called last week. They talked for forty minutes. Gary asked how the line sounded. Dennis said he didn't know — they'd moved him off the floor and into an office in Building 3 with a standing desk and a monitor that was too large and a window that faced the parking lot where the Sourcient consultants parked their rental cars.

"Can you still hear the vending machine?" Gary asked.

Dennis listened. He couldn't. Building 3 didn't have a vending machine. It had a "micro-market" with touchscreen ordering and a wellness bowl for $14.

"No," he said.

"That's how it starts," Gary said.

A note on names: The procurement platform in this story is called Sourcient AI. Read it like "source" plus "sentient." The company's tagline is "Intelligent Procurement, Autonomous Decisions." The autonomy is the selling point. The autonomy is also the problem. When you remove the human from the procurement loop, you don't just lose efficiency — you lose the phone calls, the Christmas cards, the Friday afternoon conversations where a man's voice tells you something the data never will. The AI claims sentience over the sourcing now. The process has lost the thing that made it work. Nobody at Sourcient thinks of it that way. It says so in their name.

A note on philosophy: The ancient Greeks had a thought experiment called the Ship of Theseus: if you replace every plank in a ship, one at a time, is it still the same ship? Dennis's spreadsheet has been modified in every cell since 2011. Not one original value remains. It is still Dennis's spreadsheet. The question is not whether the spreadsheet is the same. The question is whether the judgment encoded in each replacement — the weather suffixes, the conditional formatting, the knowledge of which vendor answers the phone on Fridays — survives the replacement of the man who built it. Theseus's ship sailed fine with new planks. Dennis's spreadsheet does not sail fine with new operators. The Greeks did not account for institutional knowledge. Most people don't.

This is the second installment of Knowledge Atom — a series about the quiet absurdities of the modern enterprise.

If the first installment was about what happens when you extract knowledge from the person who has it, this one is about what happens when you replace judgment with data and call it progress.

If you're reading this and thinking about your own Dennis — the person whose spreadsheet holds your company together — maybe send them a message. Not an automated one.
